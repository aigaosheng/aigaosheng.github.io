# AI Governance Brief: Italy’s AI Act Decree Takes Effect

**Today:** A thin news day with two stories that matter: Italy’s AI Act enforcement decree went live, and MEPs argued over global AI rules and who pays when AI causes harm.

## Top Stories

### 1. 🏦 **Italy’s AI Act Enforcement Decree Takes Effect**
*Cyber Security 360 · October 23, 2026*

**Bottom line:** Italy now has a working national enforcement structure for the EU AI Act, with named supervisors, inspection powers and a penalty regime.

Legislative Decree 179/2026 entered into force today. It names ACN as the market surveillance authority and AgID as the notifying authority. Banca d’Italia, Consob and IVASS supervise high-risk AI in their own sectors, and the data protection authority keeps its role under the AI Act. The decree applies the EU fine ceilings, up to €35 million or 7% of worldwide turnover for prohibited practices. It also allows non-monetary remedies for minor breaches, including a public declaration of the violation, and sets up a national regulatory sandbox run by AgID and ACN. The source notes that today’s date does not switch on every AI Act obligation: the EU timetable still governs, with stand-alone high-risk duties set for December 2, 2027.

**Why it matters:** For Asia-based fintechs and enterprises with EU customers or partners, this shows AI Act enforcement being run by national and sector supervisors, including financial regulators. Teams should confirm which authority would examine their AI systems rather than assume enforcement comes only from Brussels.

### 2. 🏦 **European Parliament Splits on How to Govern AI at Home and Abroad**
*Tech Policy Press · October 22, 2026*

**Bottom line:** MEPs broadly want international AI cooperation, including with the US and China, but remain divided on how tight Europe’s own rules should be.

In a Strasbourg debate on global AI governance, lawmakers floated ideas ranging from an arms-control-style AI restraint treaty to an IAEA-style inspection regime with mandatory pre-market testing, incident notification and liability rules. Greens and Socialists rejected leaving oversight to AI companies, while a centre-right co-rapporteur on the AI Omnibus argued that Europe must also lead in building AI to shape global rules. The Commission defended its withdrawal of the AI Liability Directive, citing resistance from national governments. Lawmakers countered that the AI Act and the revised Product Liability Directive leave gaps for victims trying to prove harm.

**Why it matters:** The liability gap is the open question for any company deploying AI in the EU: fines punish breaches but do not compensate victims. Pressure on the Commission could revive the issue, so Asia-based providers selling into Europe should track it alongside the AI Act timetable.

## Key Takeaways
- AI Act enforcement is being built at the national and sector level, with financial supervisors named in Italy’s case, while the EU still debates the shape of global rules.
- Human oversight and liability remain the live fault lines, in both enforcement design and political debate.
- Penalty regimes and inspection powers are arriving faster than the high-risk compliance deadlines, so supervisory scrutiny can begin before every obligation applies.

## What to Watch
Italy still has to issue the implementing regulation defining how ACN inspections will work and the access rules for its AI sandbox. In Brussels, watch whether Parliament’s pressure produces any Commission rethink on AI liability.
