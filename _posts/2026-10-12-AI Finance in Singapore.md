# AI and Finance in Singapore Brief — 2026-10-12

**Today:** Singapore's financial sector is confronting AI's next phase: tighter controls on autonomous systems, scrutiny of AI infrastructure financing, and new opportunities in tokenised financial markets.

## Top Stories

### 1. 📊 DBS CEO says markets are repricing AI financing risks

*The Business Times · 12 October 2026*

**Bottom line:** DBS CEO Tan Su Shan says investors are demanding stronger returns and more disciplined financing structures for AI infrastructure, raising the prospect of credit-rating pressure on weaker borrowers.

In an interview with Bloomberg Television, Tan said markets were reassessing the risks associated with the rapid expansion of AI capital expenditure. DBS had originated approximately S$25 billion (US$20 billion) in AI-related deals during 2026 to date, primarily financing hardware such as semiconductors, memory chips and data centres, while retaining only a fraction of the exposures.

**Why it matters:** AI infrastructure is becoming a significant financing opportunity for Singapore's banking sector, but loan origination does not eliminate the underlying risks. Lenders and investors will need to scrutinise borrower credit quality, cash-flow visibility, asset utilisation and the sustainability of expected AI returns as capital commitments grow.

### 2. 💳 MAS advances wholesale CBDC settlement for tokenised bills

*Fintech Singapore · 12 October 2026*

**Bottom line:** The Monetary Authority of Singapore plans to use wholesale central bank digital currency (CBDC) to settle tokenised MAS Bills, advancing the integration of digital assets with central bank money.

MAS Managing Director Chia Der Jiun outlined the plans at the INSEAD Digital Finance & Agentic AI Summit on 9 October. The initiative aims to bring tokenised assets and digital central bank money onto common infrastructure, supporting settlement finality. MAS is also working through Project Guardian and Global Layer One to address barriers to commercial tokenisation and interoperability between financial networks.

**Why it matters:** Tokenised securities require reliable settlement arrangements if they are to move beyond limited pilots into institutional markets. Combining tokenised assets with wholesale CBDC could help reduce settlement friction and support new financial-market infrastructure, although interoperability, liquidity and commercial adoption remain important challenges.

### 3. 🤖 DBS and TikTok leaders stress human judgment in AI adoption

*The Straits Times · 12 October 2026*

**Bottom line:** DBS CEO Tan Su Shan and TikTok CEO Shou Zi Chew argue that AI adoption must produce meaningful business and human outcomes, with human judgment and appropriate safeguards remaining essential.

Speaking separately at the inaugural DBS Asia Future Forward Conference in Singapore, Tan said companies need to rethink workflows rather than simply layer AI tools onto existing processes. Chew highlighted the importance of understanding AI capabilities and limitations, warning that users may grant AI agents permissions, including access to credit cards, without fully appreciating the risks.

**Why it matters:** Banks and fintech companies face pressure to demonstrate measurable returns from AI while controlling operational, cybersecurity and customer risks. As AI agents gain the ability to initiate transactions or perform tasks autonomously, financial institutions will need clearly defined permissions, effective monitoring and escalation mechanisms that preserve human oversight where necessary.

### 4. 🌐 Singapore President warns AI could reduce opportunities for fresh graduates

*Reuters · 12 October 2026*

**Bottom line:** Singapore President Tharman Shanmugaratnam warned that AI is dampening demand for entry-level jobs, particularly in sectors most exposed to automation.

Speaking at the IMF and World Bank annual meetings in Bangkok, Tharman urged governments to address the decline in opportunities for graduates entering the workforce. He also warned that AI-driven disruption could widen the economic divide between advanced and developing economies.

**Why it matters:** Singapore's financial sector relies on a steady pipeline of graduates for banking, investment, compliance, risk and technology roles. AI may improve productivity across these functions while reducing demand for some traditional junior tasks. Banks and fintech employers will need to redesign training pathways so that automation does not undermine the development of future specialist talent.

## Strategic takeaway

Singapore's AI-finance agenda is developing along three connected fronts: financing the infrastructure behind AI, modernising financial-market settlement, and governing increasingly autonomous systems.

For financial institutions, the competitive challenge is no longer simply adopting AI. It is converting AI investment into sustainable returns while maintaining credit discipline, transaction integrity and customer trust. Workforce planning will be equally important as automation reshapes the entry-level roles that traditionally develop future financial-services professionals.
