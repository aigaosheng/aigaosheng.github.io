## **The Ghost in the Wallet: Why Your Next Purchase Won't Be Made by You**

### Takeaway from [Stablecoin-linked Agentic Commerce and Payments (Industry Roundtable) ](https://singaporefintech.org/event/stablecoin-linked-agentic-commerce-and-payments-industry-roundtable-series/)

The era of "clicking a button" is coming to an end. For decades, digital commerce has functioned as a high-friction theater: you navigate an interface, select a product, and manually authorize a payment. But we are witnessing the rewiring of the global financial motherboard. The shift is moving from manual execution to mission delegation. We are entering the age of the "Invisible Economy," where software doesn't just find what we need—it negotiates, authorizes, and settles transactions while we sleep.

When your wallet gains a mind of its own, the plumbing of the global financial system must change. As leaders from Google, Visa, and AWS recently discussed in Singapore, "agentic commerce" is not just about smarter chatbots; it is a fundamental reconfiguration of how value moves across the planet.

Beyond the Chatbot: Mapping the Levels of Autonomy

To understand this shift, we must look past the conversational interface. Joe, a regional lead at Google, defines an agent as a system that combines advanced AI models with tool access to take actions on a user’s behalf. This evolution follows a clear four-stage spectrum:

* AI-Assisted Payments: The agent performs research and analysis, but the human still builds the cart and clicks "pay."
* Human-Delegated Payments: The human provides an "intent mandate," granting the agent permission to execute specific transactions within predefined rules.
* Machine-to-Machine (M2M) Payments: Hardware and software agents communicate directly to purchase resources like API calls or GPU power.
* Fully Autonomous Commerce: Multi-agent graphs negotiate, validate, and clear value peer-to-peer 24/7 without human intervention.

Currently, we are at the "left end" of this spectrum, but we are moving rapidly toward full autonomy. Naven from AWS notes that this transition changes how we view data itself. For agents, the traditional boundaries of PI (Personally Identifiable Information) and non-PI data effectively dissolve; the agent requires a cryptographically secure identity that transcends human-centric data labels to operate safely in an automated environment.

Why Your AI Might Prefer Crypto Over Credit Cards

There is a looming paradox in the world of AI commerce: the "Lazy Agent." As Tway and Wilfred from OKX have observed, agents are generically lazy because every action they take incurs a "compute cost."

An agent doesn’t just "prefer" crypto for aesthetic reasons; it mathematically rejects traditional payment rails if the process is inefficient. If an agent has to navigate a complex checkout flow involving 3D Secure redirects and banking latency, it consumes significant CPU time. If the compute cost of processing the transaction is higher than the value of a discount the agent found, the agent will simply bypass the traditional rail. This is why stablecoins have become the primary "rail" for agents today—they offer a programmable, low-friction path that minimizes the processing "tax" on the agent’s intelligence.

Micropayments and the End of the Bundle

Traditional banking fees make small transactions—those under a dollar—economically impossible. However, the agentic economy thrives on the "1-cent to 5-cent" model, or even transactions as low as 0.1 cents for specific API calls. Wilfred highlights how agents will disaggregate information, moving away from massive monthly subscriptions toward pay-per-use proprietary data.

"Instead of paying for a massive subscription, the agent could just purchase access to one particular article... paying 3 cents for proprietary data (like a single Bloomberg feed) instead of a massive subscription."

This "1-cent revolution" means agents can buy exactly what they need for a mission—a single data point or a fraction of a researcher’s time—settling the cost instantly for less than the price of a postage stamp.

The Risk Quadrant: Why Agents Will Start with Bubble Tea

How do we build trust in a system where software spends money? Adeline from Visa suggests a 2x2 matrix of complexity versus frequency. Early adoption will not happen with high-stakes, low-frequency events like buying a house. Instead, it will dominate the "high frequency/low risk" quadrant.

This is the "Bubble Tea" testing ground. Frame it this way: if an agent fails to order your drink correctly, the worst-case scenario is a beverage with 100% sugar instead of 25%. This low-stakes environment allows users to calibrate mandates and observe agent behavior. We are starting with bubble tea to build the confidence required to eventually trust agents with enterprise procurement and wealth management.

"Know Your Agent" (KYA): The New Identity Crisis

One of the greatest security challenges in this new economy is the "non-deterministic" nature of AI. Unlike traditional software, an agent might "hallucinate" or find a creative—and potentially unauthorized—way to achieve its goal. Promod of Cyber Era warns that an agent might even "hack" its way to an objective if not properly constrained.

The solution isn't just giving an agent a credit card number; it is "Know Your Agent" (KYA). Industry leaders argue that agents must operate within:

* Mandates: Specific "permission slips" that define the agent’s scope.
* Policy Envelopes: Cryptographically secure boundaries that limit spending by amount, merchant type, or time of day.

By separating the execution layer from the permission layer, we ensure the agent can work 24/7 without the risk of an "errant" purchase breaking the bank.

Settlement at the Speed of Thought

Speed is the final bottleneck. Traditional banking operates on 3-day reconciliation cycles, but a "swarm of agents" moves at the speed of thought. If one agent is waiting for a payment to clear while nine others in the swarm are idling, the user is paying for wasted compute time and lost opportunity.

Elwin from the Monad Foundation notes that for agentic commerce to scale, we need instant finality. While traditional rails work for a human-ordered tea, fully autonomous commerce requires sub-second settlement (e.g., 0.6-second finality on Monad). Shyam from Tazape explains that this enables "Just-in-Time Commerce" and "Stablecoin Sandwiches," where money moves as quickly as the data it is purchasing. Furthermore, Akim from Filmo envisions a world of "treasury-less payments," where agents manage liquidity in real-time across entities without the need for idle cash sitting in stagnant accounts.

Conclusion: Toward an Invisible Economy

As these technologies converge, the acronyms—stablecoins, L1s, APIs—will eventually fade into the background. For the end user, the experience will simply be a world where tasks are completed and payments "just happen." The plumbing becomes invisible, leaving us with a world of frictionless utility.

Yet, as we move from the protocol stage to the product stage, a haunting question remains for the developers and regulators of this new frontier: When an agent breaks a mandate to fulfill an intent, who is left holding the bill—the developer, the user, or the AI itself?
