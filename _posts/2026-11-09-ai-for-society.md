# AI for Society Brief: US Voters Reject AI Data Centers

**Today:** Fresh US midterm results and workforce data show public tolerance for AI's physical and economic footprint is thinner than the industry assumed, a signal enterprise and policy teams in Asia should watch as they plan their own AI infrastructure and workforce transitions.

## Top Stories

### 1. 👥 **US Midterm Voters Reject Nearly Every Local AI Data Center Measure**

*Fortune · November 9, 2026*

**Bottom line:** Voters rejected AI data center expansion in all but one of 41 local ballot measures across 10 US states, confirming pre-election polling that showed deep public unease about AI's infrastructure footprint.

Exit polling cited in the analysis found roughly three-quarters of voters opposed a data center in their own community, with more than half citing AI and data centers as a major factor in how they voted; most rejected measures passed by margins exceeding 70%. The piece ties the result to earlier Gallup polling showing 70% of Americans oppose a nearby AI data center, and Pew findings that Americans expecting AI to harm society now outnumber optimists by more than two to one.

**Why it matters:** The backlash raises the odds of slower, costlier US hyperscale buildout and pushes grid-upgrade and community costs onto tech companies, a risk enterprises should factor into US-anchored AI capacity plans. For Singapore and other Asian markets courting sovereign AI infrastructure investment, the contrast may make the region comparatively more attractive to compute providers seeking fewer siting fights.

### 2. 👥 **Companies Rehire AI-Replaced Workers at Higher Cost**

*Fortune · November 9, 2026*

**Bottom line:** Nearly one in five US workers replaced by AI have been asked back to their old jobs, often at higher pay and with new demands for job security and AI-use transparency.

Citing SHL and Robert Half survey data, the report finds 19.1% of US workers replaced by AI were later asked to return, with 51% of returnees demanding substantial pay increases and 42% requiring written job-security guarantees; separate tracking shows 121,401 AI-related job cuts announced through October 2026, more than double 2025's pace. SHL's Lucy Beaumont attributed the pattern to employers automating roles "before it has genuinely assessed the human capability inside it," while cases at Klarna and Ford illustrate AI-driven service and quality failures that forced rehiring.

**Why it matters:** The "AI rebounding" pattern is an early signal for enterprise and fintech leaders that premature automation of customer-facing or quality-sensitive roles carries a rehiring tax, a cautionary data point for banks and BaaS providers in Singapore, the Philippines and Indonesia weighing how fast to automate compliance, support and underwriting functions.

## Key Takeaways

- Public tolerance for AI's physical footprint (data centers, grid costs) is becoming a binding political constraint in the US, not just a technical or financing one.
- Premature workforce automation is proving reversible but costly, with rehired staff extracting higher pay and transparency commitments.
- Both trends point to the same underlying dynamic: AI deployment decisions made without public or workforce buy-in are triggering expensive course corrections.

## What to Watch

Watch whether more US states and municipalities move to replicate the ballot-measure model seen on November 3, and whether enterprise surveys from SHL, Robert Half or similar trackers show the AI-rebounding share rising beyond 19% in future readings.
