## Strategic Takeaways

**1. The AI race is becoming an infrastructure race.**
The decisive question is increasingly not simply which country produces the strongest model, but which ecosystem can secure chips, data centers, electricity, semiconductor equipment, capital, and manufacturing capacity at scale.

**2. China is compensating for chip restrictions through vertical integration.**
Huawei and other Chinese companies are pushing toward domestic GPUs, semiconductor equipment, fabs, and AI infrastructure. Export controls may slow China's access to leading-edge technology while simultaneously accelerating investment in alternatives.

**3. Southeast Asia is becoming strategically important.**
Malaysia and other regional markets can serve as both AI deployment hubs and potential pathways for compute access. This creates a new layer of competition over sovereign AI infrastructure and technology standards.

**4. The US retains major advantages, but the competitive metric is changing.**
US strengths remain concentrated in frontier-model research, advanced accelerators, software ecosystems, capital, and data-center investment. China’s advantages increasingly lie in manufacturing scale, hardware localization, cost efficiency, and industrial deployment.

**5. The strategic contest is no longer strictly bilateral.**
Countries in Southeast Asia, the Middle East, Europe, and elsewhere are gaining leverage by choosing—or mixing—US and Chinese AI technologies. The next phase of the AI race may therefore be determined as much by ecosystem adoption and international alignment as by benchmark leadership.
