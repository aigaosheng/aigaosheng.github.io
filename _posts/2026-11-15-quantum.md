# Quantum Brief: Turkey Picks IQM For National Quantum Computer

**Today:** A new sovereign quantum computing commitment out of Turkey and a materials-science fix for a stubborn qubit error source both point to the same theme — governments and labs are racing in parallel to close the gap between today's noisy hardware and tomorrow's fault-tolerant machines.

## Top Stories

### 1. ⚛️ **Turkey Commissions 54-Qubit IQM System For National Research Council**

*ShiftDelete.Net · November 15, 2026*

**Bottom line:** Turkey will build a sovereign, state-owned quantum computer inside its national science council, TÜBİTAK, using Finnish vendor IQM's hardware, targeting commissioning in 2027.

Industry and Technology Minister Mehmet Fatih Kacır announced that a 54-qubit IQM Radiance R3 system, officially named "ARF Quantum," will be installed and fully operated by TÜBİTAK, with access granted to universities, public institutions and industry through the national TRUBA computing infrastructure alongside the existing ARF supercomputer. The project is framed under Turkey's "National Technology Initiative" with an explicit goal of ranking among the world's top 15 nations in quantum computing, targeting applications from drug and materials discovery to cybersecurity while keeping data and IP under domestic control. No contract value or precise 2027 delivery date was disclosed.

**Why it matters:** Turkey joins a widening group of mid-sized economies buying sovereign quantum systems from commercial vendors rather than relying only on cloud access — a pattern also visible in South Korea's supercomputer-quantum integration plans and Singapore-linked ventures such as Horizon Quantum. For regulated industries in Asia already facing MAS- and APRA-style post-quantum migration deadlines, it is a reminder that national quantum procurement and cryptographic risk timelines are advancing on similar multi-year horizons.

### 2. 🔬 **Neon-Film Coating Cuts a Major Source of Qubit Errors**

*arXiv preprint · November 15, 2026*

**Bottom line:** A simple, non-destructive neon-film treatment meaningfully reduces two-level-system (TLS) losses, one of the main defect-driven error sources limiting superconducting qubits.

Researchers led by A. Palmer, alongside co-authors including M.C. Cassidy and S.N. Coppersmith, posted a preprint describing how depositing a solid neon film on superconducting circuits and then letting it evaporate redistributes or removes surface fabrication residue, such as polymer contamination confirmed via atomic force microscopy, without chemically altering the underlying material. Tested on NbTiN coplanar waveguide resonators, the treatment produced lasting improvements in internal quality factor of up to 4.3x on one device and 2.4x on another, reproducible across two separate dilution refrigerators and compounding with repeated treatments.

**Why it matters:** TLS losses are a persistent barrier between today's error-prone qubits and the error-corrected, utility-scale machines that vendors including IQM, Quantinuum and IBM are racing to deliver this decade. A cheap, non-destructive fabrication fix like this could be adopted broadly across superconducting roadmaps, indirectly supporting the hardware progress that regulators' post-quantum migration timelines are implicitly betting on.

## Key Takeaways

- Sovereign quantum procurement is spreading beyond the US, EU and China, with mid-sized economies like Turkey now buying state-owned systems from commercial vendors such as IQM rather than relying solely on cloud access.
- Incremental materials-science fixes, like the neon-film treatment for qubit defects, continue to chip away at the error rates separating today's noisy processors from fault-tolerant machines.
- Both government infrastructure bets and underlying hardware research are converging on the same 2027–2030 horizon that regulators such as MAS and APRA are using to set quantum-safe migration deadlines for financial institutions.

## What to Watch

Watch for TÜBİTAK's ARF Quantum system to reach its targeted 2027 commissioning, and for independent replication or peer review of the solid-neon TLS-loss results, including whether superconducting qubit vendors begin integrating the treatment into their own fabrication processes.
